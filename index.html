Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus

Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley · first published 1818, revised 1831

Summary edit

Somewhere in the Arctic ice, an English explorer named Robert Walton takes aboard a half-frozen stranger. The stranger is Victor Frankenstein, a young Genevan who once threw himself into the study of natural philosophy, and the story he tells Walton — of a secret experiment, and of what walked away from it — makes up most of the book.

Shelley nests one voice inside another: Walton’s letters to his sister contain Victor’s confession, and at its centre Victor hands the story to the being he made. It is a short novel that reads quickly, but it rewards a class that slows down, especially over the question of who deserves our sympathy, and when.

Victor brings his creature to life and immediately flees from it. Abandoned, the creature teaches himself to speak and read by secretly watching the De Lacey family, but when he finally reveals himself they drive him away. His grief hardens into revenge: Victor’s young brother William is killed, and Justine, a servant of the household, is executed for the crime.

The creature demands a companion. Victor begins one, then destroys it. In return the creature kills Henry Clerval and, on Victor’s wedding night, Elizabeth. Victor pursues him north into the ice, which is where Walton finds him. Victor dies aboard the ship; the creature appears over his body, mourns him, and leaves to end his own life.

Five discussion questions edit

  1. The story reaches us through three narrators — Walton, Victor, and the creature. Whose account do you trust most, and does the frame change how you judge Victor?[2]Session 1 · Letters and chapters 1–5

  2. Victor never gives his creation a name. What does that absence do to the way the other characters — and we, as readers — treat him?Either session

  3. The creature learns language, history, and feeling by watching the De Lacey family through a gap in a wall. What does he learn from them, and what does their response to him finally teach him?Session 2 · Chapters 11–16

  4. Walton and Victor are both ambitious men chasing discovery at great risk. Is the novel a warning against ambition itself, or against something narrower — secrecy, isolation, or refusing responsibility?Session 2 · best near the end

  5. Shelley subtitled the book The Modern Prometheus. Which parts of the Prometheus story fit Victor, and which fit the creature better?[3]Closing question · also works as an essay prompt

Key vocabulary edit

Word Wizard Words that trip readers up in this novel, in the order they tend to appear. Add two of your own from each session.

ardour
Intense enthusiasm or passion. Walton and Victor both describe their ambitions this way.
natural philosophy
The older name for what we now call the physical sciences — Victor’s field of study.
galvanism
Early experiments using electric current to make muscles twitch. Shelley mentions it in her 1831 introduction.
countenance
A person’s face, or the expression on it. Notice how often characters judge each other by it.
daemon
Spelled this way in the novel; an evil spirit. One of several names Victor uses instead of a real name.
sublime
A feeling of awe mixed with fear, often caused by vast landscapes like the Alps or the Arctic.

Themes edit

Creation and responsibility
What does a maker owe the thing it makes? Victor’s failure is less the experiment itself than what he does the morning after.
Isolation
Nearly every major character is alone at a crucial moment — Walton at sea, Victor in his workshop, the creature in the woods. Watch how solitude shapes each choice.
Nature and the sublime
Mountains, glaciers, and storms arrive at the story’s turning points. Shelley uses landscape to restore her characters, to dwarf them, and sometimes to mirror them.
Justice and blame
Justine’s trial asks who gets believed, and why. Keep it in mind as the book asks the same question of the creature.

Notes

  1. The 1831 revision is the text most paperbacks reprint. It adds Shelley’s introduction about the book’s origin during a wet summer near Geneva in 1816, and numbers the chapters straight through; the 1818 text restarts numbering in each of its three volumes. ↩
  2. Walton’s letters open and close the novel. Reading the first letter aloud is a good way to start session one. ↩
  3. In one telling, Prometheus steals fire from the gods for humanity and is punished for it; in another, he shapes the first people from clay. Both versions were familiar to Shelley’s circle. ↩

Literature circles

Six roles for a small-group discussion

In a literature circle, each student in a group of four to six takes one job for the session, then rotates next time. Every guide on the wiki is laid out so each role has something to work from.

For students

Reading it for class?

  • Start with the spoiler-free summary so you know who’s talking and where you are.
  • Keep the vocabulary list open while you read — older novels use words differently.
  • When names start to blur, check the character map instead of rereading a chapter.
  • Bring one question of your own. The best discussions start with a real one.

These guides are here to help you talk about the book, not to replace reading it. The questions have no single right answer — that’s the point.

Handbook

How to run a literature circle

A good circle isn’t a quiz and isn’t a free-for-all. It sits somewhere in between, and the facilitator’s job — teacher or student — is mostly to keep it there. These five habits come up again and again on the wiki’s talk pages.

i.

Post the reading schedule before the first page

Agree how much to read for each session and put it somewhere the whole class can see. Every guide here suggests a split at a natural break, so no one arrives having stopped mid-scene.

Decide in advance whether the ending is fair game, so students who read ahead don’t spoil it for the rest.

ii.

Assign roles, then rotate them

Give each student one role per session and rotate every time, so everyone practises summarizing, questioning, and close reading. Hand out the questions a day early so quieter students can think ahead.

iii.

Open with a round, not a question

Go around the circle once and ask everyone for a single line: a moment that stuck, a character they couldn’t stand, a page they read twice. It brings every voice in early, and it usually shows you where the real conversation is.

iv.

Send people back to the text

The most useful question in any circle is where do you see that? Opinions drift; passages anchor them. Keep books open, and allow a quiet minute while someone finds the page — it’s evidence-gathering, not dead air.

v.

End with an exit ticket

Close by asking each student to write down the one question they’re still thinking about. It makes a quick check on understanding, and it’s often the best opening for the next session.

A sample class period

Forty-five minutes, start to finish

  1. 0:00Move into circles; roles out.
  2. 0:03Summarizer’s one-minute recap.
  3. 0:06Word Wizard: words in context.
  4. 0:12Discussion Director leads questions.
  5. 0:30Passage Picker reads aloud.
  6. 0:37Connector and Character Mapper.
  7. 0:42Exit ticket: one open question.

Adapt freely. For longer blocks, add a whole-class share-out where each circle reports its best question.

Discussion formats

Four ways to shape a discussion

Most classes settle into one routine. Switching format for a single session is an easy way to wake a group up — especially halfway through a long book.

The Round

Everyone answers the same question in turn before open discussion begins. It is slower, but no one gets left out and no one dominates.

New groupsCircles of 4–6Light prep

The Close Reading

The teacher or Passage Picker chooses one or two passages, a page at most. Read them aloud, then spend the whole session there. Surprisingly often, the whole book turns out to be folded inside them.

Dense proseAny sizeSome prep

Structured Debate

Pose one arguable claim — say, “Victor is the real villain” — and have students argue a side using evidence from the text, ideally the side they don’t hold. Swap halfway, then say what you really think.

Divisive booksWhole classLight prep

The Fishbowl

A small inner circle discusses while the rest of the class sits around them, listening and taking notes on strong points and good evidence. Swap the circles halfway so everyone gets a turn inside.

Large classesWhole classLight prep

Contribute

Write or improve a guide

Guides on ReadingClub.wiki are written by teachers, librarians, and readers who have led discussions of these books. Every change is reviewed by a volunteer moderator before it appears, so the site stays accurate and classroom-safe.

  1. Find a gap

    Start with a guide marked stub or needs themes, or propose a new book. The wiki sticks to public-domain works so anyone can quote the text and print a guide without worry.

  2. Write from the template

    Every guide follows the same outline — summary, schedule, questions, vocabulary, themes, characters — so students always know where to look, whichever book they’re on.

  3. Submit it for moderation

    A volunteer moderator checks new material against the house rules and may suggest changes before it goes live. Nothing is published without review.

House rules

  • Spoilers go below the fold, clearly labelled.
  • Write your own questions. Never paste from publishers’ reading guides or study sites.
  • Cite chapters, not page numbers — editions differ.
  • One question, one idea. If it needs an “and”, it’s probably two questions.
  • Keep it classroom-appropriate, and flag mature content in the content notes.
  • Be generous to readers who didn’t love the book.
